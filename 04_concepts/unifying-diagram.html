Unifying diagram

A 3×2 grid placing every method in the course by what it explicitly represents (value function / policy / both) against what is known about the dynamics (model available / interaction only).

Where it sits

  • Unifying diagram cell: it is the diagram
  • Failure mode addressed: none
  • Course first appearance: Week 1; repeated whenever a new method is introduced

Core idea

The usual taxonomy “value-based / policy-based / actor-critic” invites the reading that these are three different families of algorithm, which they are not. The honest distinction is about which objects a method actually stores and updates. The columns record what you know about the dynamics; the rows record what the method explicitly represents.

Explicitly representsDynamics model availableNo dynamics model (interaction only)
A value function (value-based)DP, Value iteration, Policy iteration, Riccati / LQR (Wk 1–3)bandit algorithms ¶ (Wk 4), TD learning ♭ (Wk 5), Q-learning, SARSA, DQN ‡, LSTD (Wk 6–7)
A policy (policy-based)iLQR ‖ (Wk 2)Policy gradient (REINFORCE), REINFORCE with a Baseline (variance reduction) § (Wk 6, 9)
Both (actor-critic)MPC † (Wk 2, 11)Actor-critic, PPO §, DDPG, TD3, SAC (Wk 10), Dyna (Wk 11)

‖ Direct trajectory optimisation is not on the diagram, and its absence is the point. It stores an open-loop plan {u0​,…,uN−1​} (numbers, not a function of state), so by the row rule it represents nothing reusable: off the planned trajectory it has no answer. Putting it in the policy row would contradict Open-loop vs feedback, where the optimisation variable is explicitly a sequence of functions πk​:X→U. iLQR earns the cell: its backward pass leaves time-varying gains Kk​, i.e. an explicit local feedback law uk​=−Kk​(xk​−xkref​)+ukref​.

Read the model column as one spectrum of how much policy is stored: DP / LQR (a global policy, through V) → iLQR (a local law along one trajectory) → MPC (none stored, recomputed online) → direct trajectory optimisation (none at all, one plan for one x0​, hence off the grid).

† MPC represents a model and a terminal value function, but its policy is implicit, recomputed online by optimisation at every timestep and never stored. This is the awkward cell and students should be told so.

♭ TD learning is the first occupant of that cell with a Bellman object in it, and the object is Tπ rather than T: Week 5 holds the policy fixed, so no maximisation appears and the fixed point being solved is the evaluation equation. Week 6 puts the max back and the object becomes T.

‡ Q-learning, SARSA and DQN represent the state–action value Q(x,u), not V(x): the same object with one more argument, and what makes greedy action selection possible without a model. Introduced properly in Week 3.

Week 6 opens the second row. Both model-free cells of the top two rows are occupied from that session, by methods that ran on the same pendulum in the same week, which is the reason Policy gradient (REINFORCE) is taught before the deep value methods of Week 7 instead of after them. The two branches are siblings: value methods centre on a fixed-point equation and read a policy out of it by maximisation, policy methods parameterise the policy and move it up an estimated gradient of expected return. Actor-critic in Week 10 is their synthesis, and that framing needs both branches already on the table.

Week 9 tests the row rule with a method that holds a value estimate. REINFORCE with a baseline maintains bw​, and it stays in the middle row, because the rule asks a sharper question than whether a value estimate exists. The question is whether that estimate solves a fixed-point equation. bw​ is fitted by regression on returns the episodes produced, so it predicts no prediction of its own and no Bellman object is involved. The bottom row is reserved for value estimates that bootstrap, which is the same line that puts PPO there, and it is the load-bearing distinction for Week 10. Week 9 also puts the Policy gradient theorem behind the cell, and the theorem supplies a property the columns cannot show. States are weighted by the Discounted state-visitation measure of the current policy, so everything in this cell is on-policy by construction.

Week 10 fills the bottom row, and the placement is what defines the row. A Critic is a value estimate whose target contains a value estimate, which is the same test that kept Week 9 in the middle row, applied to a method that now fails it. The substitution is one line long: fit the Week 9 baseline to rt+1​+γVw​(xt+1​) instead of to the observed return, and the object stops being a regression and starts solving a fixed point of Tπ. Everything that arrives with it, PPO, DDPG, TD3 and SAC, sits in the same cell for the same reason, and the differences between them are recorded on a separate artifact, the Design-space diagram. The bottom-left cell keeps MPC, so the actor-critic row is now the one row occupied on both sides of the model column, which is the reading Week 11 needs when a learned model moves into the middle.

Week 7 changes nothing about the placement, and that is the lesson. LSTD, LSPI and DQN represent a value function and no policy, and all three learn from interaction without a model, so they join Q-learning in the shaded cell. What changed inside the cell is the representation, and the cell records what a method represents rather than how it stores it. Two methods can be neighbours here and carry entirely different guarantees, since LSTD keeps the on-policy linear guarantee that DQN gives up (Deadly triad).

¶ The Bandit problem earns the top-right cell because μ^​i​ is Q^​(x,a) evaluated at the only state there is, so the row rule is satisfied by a legitimate value function. It is the one occupied cell that approximates no Bellman object: with no successor state there is nothing to bootstrap from, so μ^​i​ is an ordinary sample average and contraction, uniqueness and the greedy-optimality argument have no operator to act on. The gap closes in one step, since μ^​←μ^​+α(r−μ^​) is the γ=0 case of the TD learning update V^(x)←V^(x)+α(r+γV^(x′)−V^(x)).

§ PPO is placed by the rule, not by reputation: it is policy-gradient by lineage, but the version everyone runs fits a value network to bootstrapped targets, so it represents both. The dividing line is bootstrapping, not “has a value function”: a Monte-Carlo baseline is variance reduction and involves no Bellman object, while a critic predicts its own next prediction and is solving a fixed-point problem.

The columns are the ends of a spectrum, not a binary. Real robotics sits in the middle: rigid-body structure known from CAD and identification, contact and friction not. A learned model lives between the columns and is made explicit in Week 11 (Model-based RL).

The critic row is Tπ, not T: a critic evaluates the policy the actor currently has, so its fixed-point equation carries no max. (SAC’s entropy term acts as a softened maximisation, which is the one footnote the row rule needs and which changes no placement, since the rule turns on Bootstrapping.)

The “none” in the policy row is a different attack on the same problem, not a weaker version of the value-based one. Policy-gradient methods never form a Bellman residual: they parametrise the policy and differentiate the expected return.

SARSA’s entry is the one students misread, since the algorithm is on-policy and its own exploration therefore enters the target it learns. At a fixed exploration rate it solves the Exploration-aware Bellman operator exactly, which is a legitimate question with a legitimate answer, and the distance between the two fixed points is measurable: 11.4% at ε=0.10 on the Week 6 swing-up when executed with exploration in place, and 0.8% in the opposite direction when both are executed greedily.

Key limitation

The grid classifies what a method represents, which is not the same as how well it works, how many samples it needs, or whether it is safe on hardware. It is an orientation device, not a selection criterion.
