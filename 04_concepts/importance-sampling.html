Importance sampling

Reweighting samples drawn under one distribution so that they estimate an expectation under another, by multiplying each sample by the ratio of the two densities.

Where it sits

  • Unifying diagram cell: cross-cutting. It is the device that lets a method in the model-free column use data its current policy did not generate.
  • Bellman object approximated: none. It is a change-of-measure identity, exact before any algorithm is built on it.
  • Failure mode addressed: it is the standard answer to failure mode 3, distribution shift, and it pays for that answer in failure mode 2, variance.
  • Course first appearance: Week 9, as the escape hatch from the on-policy hypothesis; formalized for fixed data sets in Week 12

Core idea

The Policy gradient theorem weights states by the visitation measure of the current policy, so one parameter update turns every stored trajectory into a sample from the wrong distribution. The identity below says that the old data can still answer the new question, provided each trajectory is scaled by how much more or less likely the new policy was to have produced it. The identity is exact and the resulting estimator is unbiased. Its cost is in the ratio, which is a product over the horizon.

Formal definition

For any f and any two policies with the same support,
Eτ∼pθ​​[f(τ)]=Eτ∼pθ′​​[(∏t​πθ′​(ut​∣xt​)πθ​(ut​∣xt​)​)f(τ)].
The transition kernels cancel from the ratio for the same reason they cancel in the policy gradient theorem, since both trajectory densities carry the same P(xt+1​∣xt​,ut​) at every step. The weight is therefore computable from the two policies alone, with no model. Its variance is the problem. A product of hundreds of per-step ratios is a random variable whose spread grows rapidly with the horizon and with the distance between the two policies, so the reweighted estimate is frequently noisier than collecting fresh data would have been. The support condition is not cosmetic, since a trajectory the old policy could not have produced receives an infinite weight and one the new policy cannot produce receives zero.

Connection to classical control

It is the reweighting that lets an experiment run under one controller answer a question about another, and its variance is the price of asking. A control engineer meets the same coupling in closed-loop identification, where the input is generated by the loop and what the data reveal about the plant depends on the controller in it. The practical rule that comes out of both settings is the same. Data from a nearby controller can be reused, data from a distant one cannot, and the honest way to use the identity is to keep the two policies close rather than to trust the weights.

Key limitation

Unbiasedness is recovered and usability is not. The variance of the product grows with the horizon, so the identity relieves the on-policy constraint on paper and rarely relieves it in practice at full episode length. The standard repairs all trade the exactness away. Truncating or clipping the ratios introduces bias, per-decision weighting shortens the product without removing the growth, and trust regions enforce closeness so that the weights stay near one. Week 10 introduces the third of these, and Week 12 takes up the case where no new experiment can be run at all, so the mismatch cannot be reduced by collecting more data.
